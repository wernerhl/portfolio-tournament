A Plain-Language Guide

What you’re looking at, and how to read it

This is a personal research dashboard. It does two things: it tracks how risky market conditions are right now, and it runs an ongoing experiment comparing a handful of investing strategies. It’s a tool for thinking about markets — not a tool that tells you what to buy.

Read this first

This is not financial advice, and the “BUY” labels are not recommendations for you to buy anything. They’re the output of an automated rulebook, shown so you can see what the rules say.

The competing strategies (“tiers”) are an experiment, not a track record to copy. They’ve been running for only a few months of real time.

And the system can’t anticipate sudden moves. It reads conditions that have already settled into prices, so a calm overnight reading can be followed by a down day it never saw coming. These happen regularly, and most are small. It’s a thermometer for the current climate — not a crystal ball for tomorrow. Treat everything here as a way to understand markets, not as a signal to move your own money.

1The risk gauge

Top of the page, under Regime · the dial that reads “safe” to “danger”

The big dial is the single most important thing on the page. It’s a summary of how stressed market conditions look right now, on a scale from calm to dangerous. Think of it like a weather reading: it doesn’t tell you it will storm, only how the sky looks at the moment.

Beside the dial sit two companions: the project’s standing claim — one paragraph, in its own words, on what the pull-back has and hasn’t shown — and a chart of how far each portfolio currently sits below its own peak.

The needle position and the headline word tell you the current state:

DEPLOYCalm. Conditions look benign. The strategies run at full size.
ELEVATED / CAUTIOUSMixed. Some warning signs are flashing. The strategies pull back a little.
HIGH RISK / CRISISStressed. Many warning signs at once. The strategies move toward cash and protection.
INTRADAY STRESSSomething is happening right now. A red banner appears across the top when the market is moving sharply today, faster than the daily reading can keep up with.

The one thing to remember

The main reading is calculated once a day, overnight, from the previous day’s closing prices (the fast fear gauges also refresh during the day). So a green dial in the morning reflects last night, not this minute. If a red “intraday stress” banner is showing, trust the banner over the dial — the dial is stale by then.

2The signal panel

Lower down, under Records & Signals · the grid of small cards — “23 channels”

The dial is a summary; this grid is the evidence behind it. Each small card is one signal pulled from financial markets or the economy — things like the fear index (VIX), the gap between safe and risky borrowing costs, or whether manufacturers are ordering more. Together they form the score on the dial.

The cards are grouped by what they tell you about when:

FORWARD-LOOKINGWhat markets expect is coming. These get the most weight.
CONTEMPORANEOUSWhat’s happening right now.
CONFIRMINGWhat has already happened — useful as confirmation, not warning.

To read a single card: the big number is the current value, the colour tells you whether it’s calm (green), worth watching (amber), or alarming (red), and the little bar shows how extreme it is versus its own recent history. Tapping a card opens its full history.

No single card matters on its own — any one of them can flash for ordinary reasons. The dial only moves when several line up. Don’t react to one red card.

The same section keeps the project’s records, deliberately set in smaller, quieter type: a thesis register (the stated reason for each holding, and what would prove it wrong); the v4 panel, a probability-style score that ranks days by risk but does not forecast; a status strip that appears only when the nightly pipeline had a problem; the stock scanner described in section 4; an append-only log of every change the tiers have made; a calibration chart that checks whether past probabilities meant what they said; and a calendar of upcoming economic events.

3The tournament

Second on the page, under Tournament & Book · the race chart, the leaderboard and the book

This is the experiment at the heart of the dashboard. Five portfolios (“tiers”) compete. Four start with the same notional money and are run by automated rules of increasing aggressiveness; the fifth is the author’s own real holdings. The race chart shows how each has grown or shrunk over time, and the leaderboard ranks them by percentage return — so they’re compared fairly even though the real one started with a different amount.

Capital PreservationThe most conservative. Holds the most cash.
Balanced GrowthA middle path.
Aggressive GrowthLeans into growth.
TacticalThe most active automated strategy.
Werner’s PicksThe author’s actual portfolio, for comparison.

Beneath the leaderboard sit three more panels: a treemap of what each tier holds (the area is the weight, the colour is the day’s move); a panel titled “What the overlay has shown”, which carries the project’s standing claim and the pre-registered test behind it; and the book — the author’s actual positions with cost, current price and profit or loss.

What this race is really testing

The point isn’t “which strategy is best — copy it.” The point is to study one specific question: does pulling back when the risk gauge is high actually protect you? A tier that’s “winning” today has only been running a few months, and its long-term backtest is flattered by known measurement biases. Don’t read the leaderboard as a recommendation. Read it as an experiment in progress.

4The stock view

The scanner, under Records & Signals · when you open an individual company

Opening a company from the scanner shows a “signal box” with a label like BUY or HOLD, plus an entry price, a stop-loss, and a target. Two separate scores sit at the top, and keeping them separate is the whole idea:

Business qualityHow good the company looks on the numbers (out of 50).
Trade nowWhether right now is a good moment to buy it — which can be very different.

A great company can be a poor thing to buy today if its price has already run up. That gap between the two scores is the useful part — a single blended “score” would hide it.

The honest caveat

These signals are an automated rulebook’s opinion, shown for study. Tested over many years, the stock-picking half of this system has shown no measurable skill — it did no better than simply holding a broad market fund. Whatever value the project has lies in the risk gauge’s pull-back, which has cut the depth of losses at a cost in return — not in the individual stock calls. So: interesting to look at, not something to trade your savings on.

5How to use it

The constructive part

Good uses

  • Watch what “market risk” looks like, and how it shifts week to week.
  • See how a strategy that pulls back in stress behaves versus one that doesn’t.
  • Learn how the pieces of a quantitative system fit together.
  • Use the term “elevated” or “crisis” as a prompt to slow down and think.

Don’t do this

  • Don’t buy or sell your own holdings because a label says so.
  • Don’t assume a tier’s recent returns will continue.
  • Don’t treat a green dial as “safe to invest today” — it’s last night’s reading.
  • Don’t act on one flashing card, or on any single number in isolation.

6What it honestly can’t do

The limits, stated plainly

It can’t anticipate sudden moves. It reads conditions that have already formed in prices. A move that arrives during the trading day — a piece of news, an ordinary sell-off — can blindside it. These happen regularly and are usually small, but the system won’t have seen them coming.

Its stock picks have no measurable skill. Over a long history, the individual BUY/SELL calls did no better than a plain index fund. The value, if any, is in the overall risk reading — and even that is a matter of shallower falls, not higher returns.

It’s young. The live competition has only a few months of history. The longer backtests behind it are affected by well-known measurement biases that flatter the results.

It updates slowly. The full reading refreshes once a day; only the fast fear gauges update during market hours. Between updates, what you see is a snapshot, not a live feed.

7What it has shown

The result, stated just as plainly

Not nothing — but less than the labels might suggest. Tested from 2010 to 2026 on the information that was actually available at the time, pulling back to cash when the gauge ran high roughly halved the deepest fall a buy-and-hold investor would have sat through. The best simple rule — one that trims exposure whenever the market turns choppy — cut that fall by about 30 percent. The protection had a price: the pulled-back portfolio earned about half of what the market returned over the period. It has shown no reliable advantage in returns, and on return for the risk taken it is not clearly better than the simple rules. The stock-picking half — the individual BUY and SELL calls — has no measurable skill. So what it has shown is a smoother ride for a smaller return, and nothing more.

8Glossary

The jargon, in plain words

Regime

The overall “mood” of the market — calm, nervous, or stressed. The dial is reading the current regime.

VIX

The market’s “fear index.” It rises when investors expect bigger swings. A jump in the VIX means nerves are up.

Term structure

Whether markets expect more fear soon or later. When near-term fear jumps above longer-term fear, it’s a sign of acute, immediate stress.

Drawdown

How far something has fallen from its recent peak. A “−10% drawdown” means it’s down a tenth from its high.

Overlay

The rule that moves a portfolio toward cash when the risk gauge is high, laid over the top of whatever the portfolio holds. “What the overlay has shown” is the test of that rule.

Point-in-time

Using only the numbers that had actually been published at the time, not the revised versions that arrived later. Several economic series get revised after the fact, and testing on the revised numbers flatters the results.

Tier

One of the five competing portfolios in the tournament.

Stop-loss

A pre-set price at which a rulebook would sell to limit a loss. A planned exit, not a prediction.

RSI

A gauge of whether a stock has risen or fallen unusually fast lately — “overbought” or “oversold.”

MA200

The average price over the last 200 days. A common rough line between an up-trend and a down-trend.

Cash allocation

How much of a portfolio is sitting in cash rather than invested. It rises when the risk gauge is high.
