A Plain-Language Guide

What you’re looking at, and how to read it

This is a personal research dashboard. It tracks how risky market conditions are, describes one real account in detail, and runs an experiment comparing a handful of rule-based strategies. It’s a tool for thinking about markets, not a tool that tells you what to buy.

Read this first

This is not financial advice. Every label on the site is descriptive. The position readings and the screen’s board come from a mechanical rulebook whose expectancy has not been validated. Nothing here says to buy or sell anything.

The competing strategies (“tiers”) are an experiment, not a track record to copy. They have only a few months of real time.

The system can’t anticipate sudden moves. It reads conditions that have already settled into prices. A calm overnight reading can be followed by a down day it never saw coming. It is a thermometer for the current climate, not a forecast for tomorrow.

1Dates, “pending” and “STALE”

Every panel carries a small date chip beside its title

Most panels are computed once a day, overnight, from the day’s closing prices. The chip tells you which day a panel describes.

as of 2026-09-30Current. The panel reflects the last completed trading day.
· close pendingNormal in the evening. The market has closed, and tonight’s run has not published that close yet. It is due by 09:30 ET the next trading day.
STALE · as of …Something was missed. The overnight publish was due and did not happen. The strip at the top of the page says why.
holdings as of …, manualTyped by hand. The positions were transcribed from the account summary. The tag disappears once a brokerage export is loaded.

Two kinds of panel keep older dates on purpose. Treasury yields and credit spreads come from the Federal Reserve’s data service, which publishes them the next business day, so those panels normally trail by one day. Test results and calibration charts carry the date they were produced; they are records, not daily readings.

The grey line at the top of each page (“referee: … non-blocking findings”) opens to show the nightly checker’s notes. An amber or red strip there means the night’s run had a real problem.

2Home

The brief, the news, the events board, then the regime

The daily brief. One colour per trading day, set by rules written down in advance, plus one sentence of facts. The colour describes the state of that session. It is not a forecast.

REDA stress condition: for example the regime at elevated or above, the S&P 500 down 2% or more, the VIX up 20%, the book down 3%, or a held name below both its trailing level and its 200-day average.
YELLOWSomething worth attention: for example the complacency flag, a major release or a held name’s earnings within two sessions, the 10-year yield above 5%, or a held name close to its 200-day average.
GREENNo rule fired.

The small codes under each entry (Y1, R3 and so on) name the rules that fired; hover or tap one to see the evidence. Entries are never edited. A correction appears as a new entry.

News. The last 24 hours, primary sources first: the Fed, the statistical agencies, company filings and investor-relations pages, tagged T1 primary. Press coverage follows, tagged T2 secondary. Each headline links to its source; article text is never stored.

Events. The next 45 days, week by week: scheduled releases with their impact level, and the held names’ earnings dates with the move the options market prices for them, in percent and in dollars on the position. A CLUSTER marks a day when a major release lands on a held name’s earnings date.

The regime. The dial summarises how stressed markets look, on a scale from 0 to 1. The label follows the number:

LOW RISKBelow 0.30. Conditions look benign; the strategies run near full size.
ELEVATED0.30 to 0.50. Some warning signs; the strategies hold more cash.
HIGH RISK · CRISISAbove 0.50 and above 0.70. Many warning signs at once; the strategies move further toward cash.

The label changes only when the number clears a boundary by 0.02, so it doesn’t flicker back and forth around a line. A red banner across the top means stress during today’s session, faster than the daily reading can follow; trust the banner over the dial when it shows.

Below the dial, the indicator cards are the evidence behind it, grouped as forward-looking, contemporaneous and confirming. No single card matters on its own; the dial moves only when several line up.

Reading a card. Each card shows the value with its unit (credit spreads in basis points, slopes in percentage points), then one sentence. The first half describes the level itself: whether the VIX curve is in contango or backwardation, whether the yield curve is inverted, whether a return is up or down. The second half compares the reading with its own past year: “near”, “above” or “far above its past-year norm”. A card marked Extreme (1y) sits at the far end of its own past year, not necessarily at an extreme of history. That is why each card also shows its percentile over ten years (or over the history on record, when a series is shorter): in late September the high-yield spread was far above its past-year norm yet near the middle of its longer record. Ordinary readings are never described as a crisis.

Rates stress. A strip under the cards, marked DIAGNOSTIC and not part of the regime index, shows the bond market’s volatility gauge (MOVE) and the 10-year real and nominal yields against their history. It is a monitor. Nothing on the site acts on it unless a registered test shows it adds information and Werner approves its use in writing.

3Book

One real account, described in detail

The book lists the account’s positions and cash, with the account value, the share invested, volatility, sensitivity to the market (beta) and how concentrated it is in a few themes. Every figure is descriptive; no rule drives the account.

Goals. The house goal converts the account into reais at the latest exchange rate and shows the share of the target already covered, how the picture changes with the exchange rate, and the monthly saving that would close the gap at three assumed returns. The claims register holds a claim registered in advance and measures progress against it; the claim itself is never edited.

Stress and sleeves. Fixed scenarios (the market down 20%, chips down 30% and so on) shown in dollars and as a share of the account, and how each bond or cash sleeve would change the account’s volatility and stress loss.

Position readings. Each held name against a rulebook’s levels: “ENTRY CONDITIONS 4/5” counts how many of five technical conditions hold; “EXTENDED · 21% ABOVE MA200” says how far the price sits above its 200-day average; “BELOW TRAILING LEVEL” and “BELOW RULEBOOK STOP” say a price is under a level the rulebook watches. These are observations, not instructions.

Entry state. Marked DIAGNOSTIC until its registered test reports. Every stock card (book, screen, tournament) carries one of five states, read from the last close. AVOID: the trend gate fails (the 12-month return excluding the last month is not positive, or the close is under the 200-day average). WAIT: the trend passes but there is no pullback. WATCH: a pullback is present (the close more than one ATR under the 20-day average, or RSI at or below 40, or the close in the bottom quarter of its 40-session range) but nothing has turned yet; the card shows the trigger, a close above today’s high. READY: that trigger happened, or a break below the 40-session low was recovered. READY-HALF: the same with earnings inside 20 sessions, at half size. The stop is the 40-session low less one ATR, raised to the 200-day average when that is higher; a close below it is logged and the name drops back. The size is the number of shares that loses 0.5% of the account if the stop is hit, cut so that the name stays under 40% of the book’s risk, with the account’s volatility, beta and largest risk share before and after. It is a rule’s output, not an instruction to trade.

Mean-reversion reference. The level formerly called the entry level (the higher of the 50-day average and 5% under the price). It says where the price would sit after an ordinary pullback; it is a reference, not a place to enter. The entry state above reads timing. The rulebook stop shown beside it is a separate percentage rule.

Hedge selector. Marked DIAGNOSTIC. It prices four option structures on the live chain for each held name and shows what each would do to the account under stress. It is descriptive and has no execution path.

4Bonds

The Treasury curve, credit spreads and inflation expectations, each placed against its own history (ten years where it exists; the credit-spread series on record start in 2023), plus a combined rates state marked DIAGNOSTIC. The sleeves are asset-class funds; what matters for the account is how each moves with what is already held. No panel forecasts rates.

Are you paid to take duration? The page reads each fund’s yield off today’s Treasury curve at the fund’s average maturity (adding the credit spread for corporate funds). That “curve-implied” yield is what the fund buys at today’s rates. The extra yield over three-month bills is the pickup. The breakeven rise is how far rates would have to climb over a year to wipe that pickup out, and it is compared with an ordinary day’s move in the 10-year yield. In late September a seven-to-ten-year fund picked up about 96 basis points over bills, which a rise of about 13 basis points over a year would erase: roughly three ordinary days. The figure describes the cushion; it does not predict rates. The income a fund paid over the past year is still shown, labelled as trailing, because it lags rate moves.

Rates stress. MOVE, the bond market’s counterpart to the VIX, and the 10-year real and nominal yields, each against its own history, with their 60-session change. Marked DIAGNOSTIC: a monitor, read by nothing that sizes, labels or colours anything on the site.

5Screen

A nightly ranking of about 530 companies on four factors: business quality, technical state, visibility of future revenue, and penalties for leverage and for overlap with what is already held. HELD marks names in the account. The ranking describes the model’s factors; name selection has shown no measured skill. A high rank says nothing about timing: each row also carries the entry state, its trigger, stop, size and the sessions to the next earnings (see Book), and on a phone the state sits under the ticker.

6Tournament

Four rule-based portfolios compete, from the most conservative to the most active. Each starts from the same notional amount and holds more cash when the regime reading rises. The race chart shows their growth; the leaderboard ranks them by total return after trading costs.

CAP PRESDefensive sectors only; the most cash.
BALANCEDThe full universe, a middle path.
AGGRESSIVELeans into recent strength.
TACTICALA concentrated basket, the most active.
NOT COMPARABLEThe real account’s row. It sits last and unranked until it is rebuilt from the brokerage’s transaction history, because its past values mixed real returns with bookkeeping steps.

The twins. Each rule-based tier has a twin (1c to 4c) that re-checks its holdings every day instead of once a month, trading only when a name falls well out of rank, the cash target moves by more than five points, or a weight drifts more than a quarter from its target. The twins started on 29 September 2026. They are a logged contest: the test that would judge them was checked first on the monthly tiers and could not tell them apart, so no verdict will be drawn from it.

The audit. What the tournament actually does: when each tier traded, how many names it kept, how often its picks beat the market, and how far its cash sat from target. Every trade and every holding spell is logged.

7Evidence, Register, Mistakes, System

Evidence holds the pre-registered tests and their verdicts of record, the calibration of the probability model, and the comparison of results on point-in-time against revised data.

Register holds the investment themes: each one’s claim, what would disprove it, its kill criteria and its log, plus proposed changes waiting for approval.

Mistakes is an append-only ledger of every error found, by anyone in the loop: the system, the agent that maintains it, the operator, or an advisory model. Each entry names what went wrong, what it cost, the fix, and the automatic check that now guards against it.

System shows the pipeline’s last run, the checker’s findings, the full history of daily briefs, the monthly reviews, the log of every change the tiers and the account made, and how the universe of names is built.

8How to use it

Good uses

  • Watch what market risk looks like, and how it shifts week to week.
  • See how a strategy that pulls back in stress behaves next to one that doesn’t.
  • Know which releases and earnings dates sit ahead of the account.
  • Use a YELLOW or RED brief as a prompt to slow down and think.

Don’t do this

  • Don’t buy or sell because a label or a ranking says something.
  • Don’t assume a tier’s recent returns will continue.
  • Don’t treat a green dial as “safe to invest today”; it is last night’s reading.
  • Don’t act on one flashing card, or any single number in isolation.

9What it honestly can’t do

It can’t anticipate sudden moves. It reads conditions that have already formed in prices. News during the trading day can blindside it.

Its stock selection has no measurable skill. Over a long history, the rule-based picks did no better than a plain index fund. The value, if any, is in the overall risk reading, and even that shows up as shallower falls, not higher returns.

It’s young. The live competition has a few months of history. The longer backtests behind it carry well-known biases that flatter results.

It updates slowly. The full reading refreshes once a day; only the fear gauges, the book’s values and the news refresh during market hours.

10What it has shown

Not nothing, but less than the labels might suggest. Tested from 2010 to 2026 on the information actually available at the time, pulling back to cash when the gauge ran high roughly halved the deepest fall a buy-and-hold investor would have sat through. The best simple rule cut that fall by about 30 percent. The protection had a price: the pulled-back portfolio earned about half of what the market returned. On return for the risk taken it is not clearly better than the simple rules, and the stock-selection component has no measurable skill. What it has shown is a smoother ride for a smaller return.

11Glossary

Session

One trading day, from the 09:30 ET open to the 16:00 ET close.

Regime

The overall mood of the market: calm, nervous or stressed. The dial reads the current regime.

VIX

The market’s fear index. It rises when investors expect bigger swings.

Drawdown

How far something has fallen from its peak. A −10% drawdown is a tenth below the high.

Overlay

The rule that moves a portfolio toward cash when the risk gauge is high.

Implied move

The size of the price swing around an event, such as earnings, that option prices imply. It is a price, not a forecast of direction.

Point-in-time

Using only the numbers published at the time, not later revisions. Testing on revised numbers flatters results.

Pre-registered

Written down before the data is looked at: the rule, the test and what counts as a pass. It keeps a result from being tuned after the fact.

Tier · twin

A tier is one competing rule-based portfolio. Its twin follows the same rules but re-checks them daily.

MA200 · RSI

The 200-day average price, a rough line between up-trend and down-trend; and a gauge of whether a price has moved unusually fast lately.

Cash allocation

How much of a portfolio sits in cash. It rises when the risk gauge is high.
